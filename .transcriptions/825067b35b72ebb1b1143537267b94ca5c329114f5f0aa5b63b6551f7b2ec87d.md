**Beyond Normality**

Consider a family of probability distributions involving two parameters, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, and let $F(x;\theta_1,\theta_2)$ denote the corresponding cdf. The family of normal distributions is one such family, with $\theta_1 = \mu$, $\theta_2 = \sigma$, and $F(x;\mu,\sigma) = \Phi[(x-\mu)/\sigma]$. Another example is the Weibull family, with $\theta_1 = \alpha$, $\theta_2 = \beta$, and

$$F(x;\alpha,\beta) = 1 - e^{-(x/\beta)^\alpha}$$

Still another family of this type is the gamma family, for which the cdf is an integral involving the incomplete gamma function that cannot be expressed in any simpler form.

The parameters $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are said to be **location** and **scale parameters**, respectively, if $F(x;\theta_1,\theta_2)$ is a function of $(x-\theta_1)/\theta_2$. The parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma$ of the normal family are location and scale parameters, respectively. Changing $\mu$ shifts the location of the bell-shaped density curve to the right or left, and changing $\sigma$ amounts to stretching or compressing the measurement scale (the scale on the horizontal axis when the density function is graphed). Another example is given by the cdf

$$F(x;\theta_1,\theta_2) = 1 - e^{-e^{(x-\theta_1)/\theta_2}} \qquad -\infty < x < \infty$$

A random variable with this cdf is said to have an *extreme value distribution*. It is used in applications involving component lifetime and material strength.
